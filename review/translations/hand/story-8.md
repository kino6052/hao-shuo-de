# The Emperor's New Clothes

- **English:** The emperor (literally: "the biggest person") loved clothes. He had lots of them, but he always wanted new ones (literally: "ones seen for the first time"). Two swindlers came and said: "We can make the most beautiful clothes." "Foolish people (literally: 'people whose heads aren't good') can't see these clothes." The emperor gave them lots of money. They took it, but didn't make anything at all. The emperor came to look. He couldn't see anything! But he was afraid: "If I say I can't see them, people will say I'm a fool." So he said: "How beautiful!" The emperor "put on" the clothes and went out. People on the street all said: "How beautiful!" They were afraid too. Then a little boy said: "He has no clothes on!" Everyone laughed: "It's true, he has no clothes!" The emperor knew they were right, but he kept on walking.

The blind reader's whole reading: A man, called 'the biggest person', loves clothes and wants clothes nobody has seen before. Two people come and lie to him. They say they can make the most beautiful clothes, and that people who are not clever cannot see them. He pays them a lot of money. They take it and make nothing. When he comes to look he sees nothing, but he is afraid that if he says so, people will think he is not clever. So he calls the clothes beautiful and puts on the clothes that do not exist. He goes out. The people on the road also say the clothes are beautiful, because they are afraid too. Then a little boy says the man has no clothes on. Everyone laughs and agrees. The man knows they are right, but he keeps on walking. (unsure: 'Zui da-de ren', literally 'the biggest person', appears all through the text. I read it as the most important person, the one at the top, like a ruler. It could also mean the physically largest person or the oldest. Sentence 3, 'di-yi ci kan-dao-de yifu': the gloss gives 'floor' for di, but di-yi is the ordinal 'first'. I read the phrase as clothes never seen before, so new clothes. It could also mean clothes he himself sees for the first time. Sentences 7 and 13, 'tou bu hao', literally 'head not good': I read it as not clever or stupid. Taken literally it could mean a sick or hurt head. Sentence 7 does not say what such people cannot see. From sentence 6 I take it to be the clothes. In sentence 15 I read 'mei-you-de yifu' as clothes that do not exist. In sentence 19 the gloss splits nan-hai-zi into 'difficult-still-...', but as one word it means boy. In sentence 22 the gloss gives 'facing' for dui. Here it can only mean 'correct'. In sentence 23, 'hai zai zou' says he is still walking, carrying on. The text gives no reason why he keeps going.)
